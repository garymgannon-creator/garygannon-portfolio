Who owns the work after AI arrives?

“AI does not implement itself. Tools create value only when leaders closest to the work can define the real problem, redesign the workflow, verify consequential outputs, coach adoption, manage exceptions, and remain accountable for outcomes.”

This confidential self-assessment helps leaders identify their organization’s practical readiness to implement AI through human judgment, operating discipline, workforce capability, and responsible governance.

Designed for: Operations · HR · Safety/Security · Compliance · Workforce Development · Multi-site and 24/7 Environments

What this is—and is not

  • A structured reflection and conversation starter for executive, HR, operations, safety, security, compliance, and workforce leaders.
  • A way to identify readiness gaps before an AI pilot or broader implementation.
  • Not a legal analysis, ISO certification assessment, technology selection tool, guarantee of performance, or substitute for qualified legal, technical, privacy, cybersecurity, or employment advice.

How to use this assessment

  1. Assess six organizational foundations: Rate the practical conditions that determine whether AI can be adopted safely and used in real work.

The Six GCG Implementation Foundations

Rate the practical conditions that determine whether AI can be adopted safely. Provide one answer per statement.

Please answer all foundation questions before continuing. Missing questions are highlighted.
Foundation 1

Value and mission

Why it matters: AI efforts are more likely to produce useful outcomes when they begin with a meaningful operating, customer, workforce, safety, quality, risk, or compliance problem—not a tool purchase.

Foundation 2

Accountable operating leaders

Why it matters: AI adoption stalls when no credible business or frontline leader owns the changed work after a pilot ends.

Foundation 3

Human-owned workflow design

Why it matters: AI should support better work, not create unclear decisions, hidden handoffs, or unaccountable automation.

What is a "human-owned decision"?

A decision that a responsible person—not an automated system—must make or approve because it has meaningful consequences for people, safety, compliance, rights, service, money, or reputation.

Foundation 4

Data, controls, and evidence

Why it matters: Useful AI depends on trustworthy information, appropriate access, visible sources, review discipline, and records that support accountability.

Foundation 5

Learning and reinforcement

Why it matters: Training creates awareness; managers, incentives, practice, feedback, and workflow reinforcement make new behavior stick.

Foundation 6

Scale and measurement

Why it matters: An isolated AI pilot is not an operating capability. Organizations need measures, learning loops, and a disciplined method for reuse and local adaptation.

Can your frontline leaders make AI real?

“Tech-capable frontline leaders” are not necessarily technical specialists. They are credible domain and operating leaders who understand the work, can use data and technology thoughtfully, can lead people through changed workflows, and remain accountable for outcomes.

Please answer all leadership questions before continuing. Missing questions are highlighted.
Dimension 1

Domain and workflow ownership

Leaders understand the actual work, including exceptions, workarounds, handoffs, risks, service requirements, and customer or employee impacts.

Dimension 2

Problem definition and value judgment

Leaders begin with the operating problem and intended outcome before selecting or promoting an AI tool.

Dimension 3

Data, technology, and verification fluency

Leaders can use AI and data thoughtfully without treating outputs as automatically correct.

What is a "human review"?

A meaningful check by a person with the knowledge and authority to question, correct, override, or stop an AI-supported output. It is not a rubber stamp.

Dimension 4

Human accountability and risk judgment

Leaders know which decisions must remain human-owned and can manage exceptions, escalation, and consequential risk.

What is an "escalation trigger"?

A defined condition requiring the issue to be reviewed by a qualified person or higher authority, such as uncertainty, conflicting evidence, a safety concern, sensitive information, suspected bias, or a result outside normal limits.

Dimension 5

Change leadership, learning, and adoption

Leaders can turn a changed workflow into clear expectations, practice, coaching, feedback, and accountable daily behavior.

Do not choose only the most technical people. Consider credible leaders closest to high-consequence workflows and real employee adoption.
